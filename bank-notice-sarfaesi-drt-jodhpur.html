Bank Notice Under SARFAESI at Jodhpur: 13(2) Reply, Section 14, DRT Jaipur

A bank’s notice under the SARFAESI Act, 2002 is not the beginning of a case. It is the start of a clock, and so is every paper that follows it. This page sets out each stage for a borrower whose property lies in Jodhpur or the Marwar districts, the number of days that stage allows, and the counter — a branch, a Collectorate, a Tribunal at Jaipur, this Bench — at which it is answered.

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur November 2026

In short. A Section 13(2) notice gives sixty days to pay. Inside those days a written objection goes in under Section 13(3A), which the bank must answer with reasons in fifteen days. Possession comes next, through the District Magistrate of the district where the asset lies (Section 14). The borrower’s remedy is an application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17 within forty-five days of that measure — for Rajasthan, the Tribunal at Jaipur, not the High Court here.

At a glance — the stage, the clock, the counter
  • Section 13(2) demand notice — sixty days from the date of notice to discharge the liabilities in full; the account must already stand classified as a non-performing asset. Counter: the bank.
  • Section 13(3A) objection — filed inside those sixty days; the secured creditor must communicate reasons for non-acceptance within fifteen days. It does not open the Tribunal. Counter: the bank.
  • Section 13(4) measures — possession, lease, assignment or sale of the secured asset; take-over of management; notice to a debtor of the borrower under clause (d).
  • Section 14 possession — written request plus an affidavit of nine declarations to the District Magistrate of the district where the asset lies; order within thirty days, extendable on recorded reasons to not more than sixty in the aggregate. Counter: the Collectorate.
  • Section 17 application — forty-five days from the date the measure was taken, before the Debts Recovery Tribunal; for Rajasthan, the Tribunal at Jaipur. Disposal in sixty days, four months in all (Section 17(5)).
  • Section 13(8) redemption — the whole dues with costs, charges and expenses, tendered before the date of publication of the auction, quotation, tender or private-treaty notice.
  • Section 18 appeal — thirty days from receipt of the Tribunal’s order, with a deposit of fifty per cent of the debt due, reducible for reasons recorded in writing to not less than twenty-five per cent.
  • Outside the Act — a security interest created in agricultural land (Section 31(i)); a financial asset not exceeding one lakh rupees (clause (h)); dues below twenty per cent of principal and interest (clause (j)).

Why the file that arrives here is usually two months old

The folder that reaches the chamber at Ratanada in a bank matter almost never arrives on the day the first notice did. It arrives after the branch visit, after the one-time-settlement conversation, after a relative has said the High Court is five minutes away and will stop everything. By then the sixty days of Section 13(2) are gone, the objection that should have been filed inside them was never filed, and what the party now wants is a stay of an auction whose notice has already been published. The Act is unusually indifferent to good faith and unusually attentive to dates. Which is the whole reason this page is arranged by clock and not by argument.

Two structural facts explain most of the surprise. First, Section 35 gives the Act effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law, and Section 34 bars a civil court from entertaining any suit or proceeding in respect of a matter the Tribunal is empowered to determine — and bars any court from granting an injunction against action taken under the Act. The civil court at Jodhpur is therefore not an option, however near it is. Second, the Tribunal that is the option does not sit in this city.

The sixty days of Section 13(2) are for a reply, not for waiting

Section 13(2) is conditional on two things, and both are worth checking before anything is conceded: the borrower must be in default of a secured debt or an instalment of it, and the account must have been classified by the secured creditor as a non-performing asset. Only then may the creditor require payment in full within sixty days from the date of notice, failing which the Section 13(4) rights arise. Section 13(3) requires the notice itself to give details of the amount payable and of the secured assets intended to be enforced — a notice silent on the assets is not the complete document the section contemplates. What the full English treatment of this stage looks like, including the bar in Section 13(13) on transferring the secured asset after the notice without written consent, is on SARFAESI Section 13(2) notice: what a borrower can actually do.

Section 13(3A): the one step that is cheap, local and usually skipped

On receipt of the 13(2) notice the borrower may make a representation or raise an objection; the secured creditor must consider it, and if it is not accepted, must communicate the reasons within fifteen days. That sub-section was inserted in 2004 after Mardia Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India (Supreme Court, 8 April 2004, (2004) 4 SCC 311), and it is the borrower’s only statutory hearing before possession. It costs a registered post receipt. It also does the quiet work that matters later: the objection is where the quantum, the NPA date, the service of the notice and the character of the security are first put on paper, and the bank’s reply — or its failure to reply in fifteen days — becomes an annexure to the Section 17 application. The proviso to Section 13(3A) is equally important to know in advance: neither the reasons communicated nor the likely action confers any right to apply to the Tribunal. So the objection is a record, not a remedy. File it anyway, and keep the postal proof.

Section 14: the possession order is made at the Collectorate, not at a court

Where physical possession is wanted, the secured creditor applies in writing to the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or the District Magistrate within whose jurisdiction the secured asset or its documents are situated or found. For a house at Shastri Nagar or a shop near Sojati Gate that is the District Magistrate at Jodhpur; for a cold store in Pali or a plot in Barmer it is that district’s. The application must be accompanied by an affidavit of the authorised officer making nine declarations — among them the NPA classification, service of the sixty-day notice, and that the borrower’s objection was considered and reasons communicated. The order is to be passed after satisfying the contents of that affidavit within thirty days, and for reasons beyond his control recorded in writing, within a further period not exceeding sixty days in the aggregate. Section 14(1A) lets him authorise a subordinate officer to take possession; Section 14(2) permits such force as in his opinion is necessary; Section 14(3) puts his act beyond question in any court or before any authority. The practical consequence is the one borrowers resist most: this is not a hearing. In R.D. Jain and Co. v. Capital First Ltd. (Supreme Court, 27 July 2022) the step was held to be ministerial, with no adjudication of the borrower’s contentions, and exercisable by an Additional District Magistrate. A tenant’s claim is no different — Balkrishna Rama Tarle v. Phoenix ARC Pvt. Ltd. (Supreme Court, 26 September 2022) sends it to Section 17 as well. Objections raised at the Collectorate are therefore not lost; they are simply filed at the wrong counter. The stage is set out at length in the bank took possession through the District Magistrate under Section 14 — was that legal?

Section 17: forty-five days, and a Tribunal at Jaipur

This is the real remedy, and it lies only against a measure under Section 13(4) — not against the 13(2) notice, and not against the rejection of the representation. The application goes to the Debts Recovery Tribunal within forty-five days from the date on which the measure was taken. For a borrower in this part of Rajasthan the Tribunal is at Jaipur: the application is drafted here and heard there, and clients from western Rajasthan are regularly taken aback that a Jodhpur property will be argued in Jaipur. Section 17(1A) does give a choice of Tribunal — where the cause of action wholly or in part arises, where the secured asset is located, or where the branch maintaining the account is — which is a live question for a Jodhpur trader whose account runs from a metro branch. There is no deposit to be heard: the seventy-five per cent condition once in Section 17(2) was struck down in Mardia Chemicals. The Tribunal may declare the recourse invalid and restore possession or management under Section 17(3), and must dispose of the application within sixty days, the total pendency not exceeding four months under Section 17(5). The filing mechanics, the caveat the bank may already have lodged under Section 18C, and what a stay actually requires are on how a Section 17 application is filed in the DRT.

Auction: the publication date is the date everything is measured from

Section 13(8) as substituted in 2016 keeps the asset out of a lease, assignment or sale if the whole of the dues together with all costs, charges and expenses is tendered at any time before the date of publication of the notice for public auction, for inviting quotations or tender, or for a private treaty. After that publication the right of redemption is extinguished — Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors (Mumbai) Pvt. Ltd. (Supreme Court, 21 September 2023). So a family arranging funds from a sale of other land, which in Marwar is rarely a quick exercise, is working against the newspaper and not against the auction date printed in it. What remains after publication is an attack on the process itself under Section 17, and defects in valuation, reserve price and the sale notice are where those applications are won. Both routes, with the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002 that govern the sale, are set out on can a bank auction be stopped? Section 13(8) and the DRT.

If the Tribunal’s order goes against you: Section 18 and its deposit

An appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal within thirty days from the date of receipt of the Tribunal’s order — from receipt, not from pronouncement. The second proviso to Section 18(1) is the hurdle: no appeal shall be entertained unless the borrower has deposited with the Appellate Tribunal fifty per cent of the amount of debt due from him as claimed by the secured creditors or as determined by the Tribunal, whichever is less; the third proviso allows that to be reduced, for reasons recorded in writing, to not less than twenty-five per cent. It cannot be waived altogether — Narayan Chandra Ghosh v. UCO Bank (Supreme Court, 18 March 2011) — and the limit was reaffirmed on the parallel provision in Kotak Mahindra Bank Pvt. Ltd. v. Ambuj A. Kasliwal (Supreme Court, 16 February 2021). Where both a claimed figure and a determined figure exist, the lower governs, so both must be placed on record. The appeal stage is treated separately in the DRT has dismissed my application — can I appeal under Section 18?

When this Bench is, and is not, the answer

The Rajasthan High Court’s Principal Seat is in this city and takes matters from Jodhpur along with Barmer, Jaisalmer, Pali, Jalore and Sirohi, so a petition under Article 226 is the instinctive first move for a borrower here. It is also the commonest wasted month. United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon (Supreme Court, 26 July 2010) holds that a High Court will ordinarily not entertain an Article 226 petition where an effective statutory remedy exists, and applies that with greater rigour to the recovery of bank dues. Article 226 is not taken away by Section 34 — nothing in a statute can take it away — but it is exercised sparingly, and in these matters it survives where there is a question of jurisdiction, where the Act does not apply to the security at all, or where a mandatory requirement has simply not been performed. The general shape of the jurisdiction is set out on writ petitions under Articles 226 and 227; which of the forums at Jodhpur takes which matter is on which court in Jodhpur hears your matter, and the chamber page is advocates in Jodhpur and which court hears your matter.

Two checks to make before any of the above

Before a single day is spent on strategy, two questions decide whether the Act applies to this borrower at all. Is the security agricultural land? Section 31(i) excludes any security interest created in agricultural land, and in the Marwar districts a sanction secured on khatedari land is not unusual; the same section excludes a financial asset not exceeding one lakh rupees (clause (h)) and any case where the amount due is less than twenty per cent of the principal amount and interest on it (clause (j)). If the Act does not apply, the enforcement is without jurisdiction, and that goes in first. And is the person served a guarantor? Section 2(1)(f) makes a borrower of anyone who has given a guarantee or created a mortgage or pledge as security for financial assistance granted to another, so a guarantor is served, and a guarantor also applies under Section 17 in his own right — and carries the Section 18 deposit if he appeals. One more thing worth remembering on timing: Section 36 bars the measures altogether unless the claim was made within the period of limitation under the Limitation Act, 1963, which is a defence on an old account and not merely a technical plea.

What to bring to a first meeting

  • Loan sanction letter and the security documents
  • Statement of account, and the NPA intimation if any
  • Every notice — 13(2), 13(4), Section 14 — with its envelope
  • The newspaper auction notice, complete, with its date
  • Valuation report or reserve price paper, if received
  • Jamabandi and khasra, where the security is land

The paper without which nothing can be advised is the envelope. The forty-five days of Section 17 run from the date the measure was taken, and the thirty days of Section 18 from the date the order was received, so the postal date is not a formality in these matters — it is the pleading. Questions asked across the table at Ratanada

The bank has sent a 13(2) notice for my Jodhpur property. Can I file in the High Court here?

A notice under Section 13(2) is a demand, not a measure, and the Tribunal’s door does not open on it at all — Section 17(1) lies against a measure taken under Section 13(4), and the Explanation to Section 17(1) says the bank’s communication of reasons for rejecting your representation does not by itself entitle you to apply. The High Court at Jodhpur is close by and the Tribunal is at Jaipur, which is why the writ petition is still the commonest false start in these matters. Article 226 is not excluded, but in United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon (Supreme Court, 26 July 2010) it was held that a High Court will ordinarily not entertain a petition under Article 226 where an effective statutory remedy exists, and with greater rigour where the dues of a bank are concerned. What the sixty days are for is a reply, not a petition.

Where is the Debts Recovery Tribunal for a borrower in Jodhpur, and how long do I have?

The Tribunal that hears Rajasthan matters sits at Jaipur, so an application from Jodhpur, Pali, Barmer, Jaisalmer, Jalore or Sirohi is prepared here and heard there. Section 17(1) allows forty-five days, counted from the date on which the measure was taken — not from the date you learnt of it, and not from the date of the 13(2) notice. Section 17(1A) then gives three alternative Tribunals: where the cause of action wholly or in part arises, where the secured asset is located, or where the branch maintaining the account is. A trader whose mortgaged shop is in Jodhpur but whose working-capital account is run from a branch in another State therefore has a choice to make, and it has to be made before the forty-five days are spent.

My loan is secured on agricultural land in a Marwar village. Does SARFAESI apply at all?

Section 31(i) says the provisions of the Act shall not apply to any security interest created in agricultural land. Two other clauses of the same section matter here as well: clause (h) excludes a security interest securing a financial asset not exceeding one lakh rupees, and clause (j) excludes any case in which the amount due is less than twenty per cent of the principal amount and interest on it. Whether the land is agricultural is decided on its character and record, which is why the jamabandi and the khasra entry are asked for at the first meeting in a bank matter just as they are in a revenue matter. If the Act does not apply, the whole enforcement — notice, possession, auction — is without jurisdiction, and that is a defence worth taking before any other.

I only signed as a guarantor. Why have I been served?

Because Section 2(1)(f) defines a borrower as any person who has been granted financial assistance by a bank or financial institution, or who has given any guarantee, or created any mortgage or pledge as security for financial assistance granted to someone else. A guarantor and a third-party mortgagor are borrowers for the purposes of this Act. That cuts both ways: the demand and the enforcement reach them, and so do the remedies — a guarantor may apply under Section 17 in his own right, and if he appeals under Section 18 the pre-deposit applies to him as it does to the principal borrower.

The auction notice has been published. Is it too late to pay and keep the property?

For redemption under Section 13(8), yes. As substituted in 2016 the sub-section protects the asset only where the whole of the dues together with all costs, charges and expenses is tendered before the date of publication of the notice for public auction, or for inviting quotations or tender, or for a private treaty. Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors (Mumbai) Pvt. Ltd. (Supreme Court, 21 September 2023) treats the right of redemption as extinguished on that publication. What survives after publication is different in kind — an application under Section 17 attacking the measures and the sale process itself, on which the Tribunal may stay the sale, declare the recourse invalid under Section 17(3) and restore possession. The date on the newspaper notice is the date to work backwards from.

Bank ne notice bheja hai aur Jodhpur me makaan hai — pehle kya saath laana hai?

Loan sanction letter, statement of account, aur sabhi notices — 13(2), 13(4), Section 14 ka aadesh — apne lifaafe (envelope) ke saath, kyunki service aur taarikh wahin se sabit hoti hai. Akhbaar me chhapa auction notice ki poori copy, date ke saath. Agar valuation report ya reserve price ka kagaz mila hai to woh bhi. Gehan (mortgage) ki property agar krishi bhoomi hai to jamabandi aur khasra. Jis ek kagaz ke bina kaam rukta hai woh notice ka lifaafa hai: Section 17 ke pentaalis (45) din us measure ki taarikh se chalte hain, aur woh taarikh kaagaz se hi tay hoti hai.
