Child Custody Laws in India: Best Interest of the Child Principle

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur April 2026

In short: No parent in India owns a right to custody. Section 13 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 and Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 make the child's welfare the paramount consideration. A child under five ordinarily stays with the mother. The petition goes to the Family Court where the child ordinarily lives, and the order can be changed later.

Child custody in India is governed by a combination of personal law and secular law. For Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 (HMGA) is the primary statute. The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 (GWA) is a general secular statute that applies to all communities and supplies the procedure. In matrimonial proceedings under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, the court can pass interim and final custody orders under Section 26. Since the Family Courts Act, 1984, every one of these disputes in Jodhpur goes to the Family Court: clause (g) of the Explanation to Section 7 places "the guardianship of the person or the custody of, or access to, any minor" squarely within its jurisdiction.

The welfare test, as the statute actually words it

Section 13(1) HMGA says the welfare of the minor "shall be the paramount consideration" when a court appoints or declares a guardian, and Section 13(2) goes further: nobody is entitled to guardianship, whatever the Act or the marriage law says, if the court thinks it will not be for the child's welfare. Section 17 GWA tells the court what to look at — the age, sex and religion of the minor, the character and capacity of the proposed guardian, nearness of kin, the wishes of a deceased parent, and the existing relations between the child and the person asking for custody. Sub-section (3) adds that if the child is old enough to form an intelligent preference, the court may consider it.

That last clause is underused. In Nil Ratan Kundu v. Abhijit Kundu (2008) the Supreme Court set aside a custody order partly because the trial court never called the boy to find out what he wanted, and partly because it had not weighed the allegations of cruelty surrounding his mother's death against the father who now sought custody. The lesson for a parent preparing a case is plain: the court weighs the whole picture of the child's life, not the parent's legal title.

In practice the factors that decide a contested file are concrete: who has actually been doing the school run and the doctor visits, where the child has been living and studying for the last year or two, whether either home has a history of violence or drinking, and whether the parent seeking custody can show a workable daily routine rather than a larger income. A higher salary helps less than people expect. Maintenance can equalise money. It cannot replace care.

A child under five, and the mother as guardian

Section 6(a) HMGA names the father, "and after him, the mother", as natural guardian of a boy or unmarried girl, but its proviso says the custody of a minor who has not completed five years "shall ordinarily be with the mother". In Roxann Sharma v. Arun Sharma (2015) the Supreme Court restored interim custody to the mother and said the Act postulates that a tender-aged child stays with her unless the father discloses cogent reasons showing the child's welfare would be jeopardised. The burden of displacing the ordinary rule is on the father.

The word "after" in Section 6(a) caused trouble for mothers for decades — schools, banks and registry offices treated it as meaning "after the father's death". In Githa Hariharan v. Reserve Bank of India (1999) the Supreme Court read it as "in the absence of", which covers a father who is away, indifferent to the child, or unable to act. A mother bringing up a child on her own after separation can therefore act as natural guardian while the father is alive.

Where to file, and what to ask for first

  • Pending divorce or judicial separation: ask the same court for interim custody and access under Section 26 HMA. Do it early. The section allows orders "from time to time", including after the decree, and its proviso requires the court to try to decide an application for the child's maintenance and education within sixty days of notice.
  • No matrimonial case pending: a petition under Sections 7 and 25 GWA before the Family Court of the place where the minor "ordinarily resides" — the test in Section 9(1). A child who has lived and gone to school in Jodhpur is dealt with in Jodhpur even if the other parent has moved to another State.
  • The child has been taken away: Section 25(1) GWA lets the court order the ward's return if that is for the ward's welfare. Where the child is being held by someone with no legal right at all, a habeas corpus petition before the High Court is maintainable — that was the holding in Tejaswini Gaud v. Shekhar Jagdish Prasad Tewari (2019), where the Court handed the child back to the father, the surviving natural guardian, from maternal relatives who had cared for her during the mother's illness.

One point that surprises many fathers: after the 2010 amendment, Section 19(b) GWA bars the court from appointing an outside guardian of the person of a minor whose father or mother is living and is not unfit. A grandparent who wants legal guardianship over a surviving parent must therefore prove that parent unfit, not merely less comfortable.

The child's property is a separate question

Custody decides where the child lives. It does not decide who may sell what the child owns. In Rajasthan families that second question often matters more: a father dies and leaves a share in khatedari land or a plot in the town, and within a year somebody asks the mother to sign a sale deed "for the child's benefit".

Section 8(2) HMGA forbids the natural guardian, without the previous permission of the court, to mortgage, sell, gift or exchange any part of the minor's immovable property, or to lease it for more than five years. Section 8(4) allows permission only "in the case of necessity or for an evident advantage to the minor", and the application is decided under the GWA procedure for Section 29 permissions. A sale made without permission is not void. Under Section 8(3) it is voidable at the instance of the minor. Article 60 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives the child three years from attaining majority to sue to set it aside. Buyers who know this pay less, or do not buy at all.

Section 6 itself has a carve-out. Natural guardianship over property excludes the minor's "undivided interest in joint family property". That share is managed by the karta of the family, not by the mother or father as guardian, and it is a frequent source of confusion when the family's ancestral land is still recorded jointly.

Access, visitation and changing an order

Even where primary custody goes to one parent, the other parent is ordinarily given access. In Vivek Singh v. Romani Singh (2017) the Supreme Court discussed what psychologists call parental alienation syndrome — a child kept by one parent and turned against the other — as a harm to the child in itself, and moved the child to the mother, noting that any presumption for either parent can be tested only after both are given a level playing field. In Yashita Sahu v. State of Rajasthan (2020), a habeas corpus matter that went up from this High Court, the Court said that where distance makes regular meetings hard, video calling is the best available form of contact and fixed a daily calling schedule.

No custody order is final in the way a money decree is. Section 26 HMA lets the court "revoke, suspend or vary" its orders, and a changed situation — a relocation, a remarriage, a new school, an older child's settled wish — is a ground for a fresh application.

Cross-border custody, and the appeal

India has not signed the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction. There is no treaty route to an automatic return order. A foreign court's custody order is taken into account but does not bind: in Ruchi Majoo v. Sanjeev Majoo (2011) the Supreme Court held that such an order is one factor and cannot override the Indian court's own assessment of the child's welfare.

Against a final judgment or order of a Family Court, Section 19 of the Family Courts Act gives an appeal to the High Court on facts and law within thirty days. For the Family Courts in Jodhpur and the districts of western Rajasthan that means the Principal Seat of the Rajasthan High Court at Jodhpur. There is no appeal from an interlocutory order or from an order passed with the consent of the parties. Read a consent arrangement on custody carefully before signing it.

Questions we are asked

Does the mother automatically get custody of a young child, and can a father ever get custody?

For a Hindu child the proviso to Section 6(a) HMGA says custody of a minor below five years shall ordinarily be with the mother, and Roxann Sharma v. Arun Sharma (2015) applied it squarely. But "ordinarily" is not absolute — a father can obtain custody of even a young child by showing the existing arrangement is against the child's welfare, such as neglect, instability or an inability to provide care. A father's case has to be built on the welfare factors, and for an older child on the child's own settled home and schooling, not on the natural-guardian label alone.

Can grandparents or other relatives keep custody instead of a surviving parent?

Only if the parent is shown to be unfit or the child's welfare clearly requires it. Section 19(b) GWA bars appointing an outsider as guardian of the person while a fit father or mother is alive. In Tejaswini Gaud v. Shekhar Jagdish Prasad Tewari (2019) maternal relatives had looked after the child during the mother's illness, yet after her death the Supreme Court upheld the High Court's order returning the child to the father, with access for the relatives. Care given by relatives earns them access and consideration; it does not displace a fit parent.

The other parent is not letting me meet my child — what can I do?

Apply to the Family Court, or to the matrimonial court under Section 26 HMA, for a structured visitation schedule — fixed days, holidays and, where distance makes meetings hard, video calls, which Yashita Sahu v. State of Rajasthan (2020) treated as the best available contact. Where an existing visitation order is wilfully defied, the remedy is enforcement or contempt, not self-help. Keep a dated record of every denied visit before you move the court.

Where do I file a custody petition, and does a foreign court's custody order bind an Indian court?

A guardianship petition lies, under Section 9 GWA, before the District Court — in practice the Family Court — of the place where the minor ordinarily resides. During a pending divorce, interim and final custody can also be sought under Section 26 HMA in the matrimonial court itself. A foreign order does not oust the Indian court: Ruchi Majoo v. Sanjeev Majoo (2011) holds it is one factor, and the Indian court decides the child's welfare for itself.

Is a custody order final, or can it be changed later?

No. Section 26 HMA lets the court vary its orders from time to time, and a material change of circumstances — relocation, remarriage, a change in schooling or the child's settled wishes, or breach of the terms — is a ground to apply again. As far back as Rosy Jacob v. Jacob A. Chakramakkal (1973) the Supreme Court said children "are not mere chattels", and welfare, not the finality of an earlier order, governs.
