NDPS Act Defence: Key Strategies and Recent Supreme Court Judgments

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur January 2026

The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 (NDPS Act) is one of the most stringent penal statutes in India, and the quantity seized decides almost everything. Under the main offence sections (Section 15 for poppy straw, 18 for opium, 20 for cannabis, 21 for manufactured drugs and 22 for psychotropic substances), a small quantity carries rigorous imprisonment up to one year, or a fine up to ten thousand rupees, or both; that ceiling was six months until the 2014 amendment. Above small but below commercial quantity, the ceiling is ten years. A commercial quantity carries not less than ten and up to twenty years, with a fine of one to two lakh rupees. Under Section 31A, a person already convicted of a commercial-quantity offence (or one under Section 19, 24 or 27A) who is convicted again of dealing in the large quantities listed in its Table can be sentenced to death; since 2014 that is an option for the court, not a fixed sentence. The quantity also decides whether the Section 37 bail bar applies at all.

Searches on prior information — Sections 42 and 43: Section 42 governs entry and search of a building, conveyance or enclosed place. An officer acting on information given by someone else must take it down in writing, and if he searches between sunset and sunrise without a warrant or authorisation, he must also record the grounds of his belief. Under Section 42(2) a copy of either goes to his immediate official superior within seventy-two hours. The Constitution Bench in Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana (2009) held that total non-compliance with Sections 42(1) and 42(2) is impermissible, while delayed compliance with a satisfactory explanation for the delay is acceptable. Section 43 is a separate power: it covers seizure in a public place or in transit, and a public conveyance counts as a public place. It contains no requirement to write the information down first. So the first question on any seizure memo is where the recovery happened, because that decides which of the two sections the officer is held to.

Personal search — Section 50: Before an officer searches a person under Section 41, 42 or 43, Section 50(1) entitles that person, if he so requires, to be taken to the nearest Gazetted Officer or Magistrate for the search. State of Punjab v. Balbir Singh (1994) treated a breach as vitiating the trial itself. The Constitution Bench in State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh (1999) put it differently, and its view governs: failure to tell the person of this right may not vitiate the trial, but it renders the recovery suspect and vitiates the conviction where the conviction rests only on the article recovered from his person in that search. Since 2001, Section 50(5) lets the officer search on the spot where he has reason to believe the person would otherwise part with the contraband, but Section 50(6) then requires him to record his reasons and send a copy to his superior within seventy-two hours.

Conscious possession and the statutory presumptions: The first instinct in a defence is to say the accused did not know what was in the bag, but the Act puts that burden on the accused. Section 35 requires the court to presume a culpable mental state, which includes knowledge, and the accused must prove its absence beyond reasonable doubt, not merely on a balance of probability. Section 54 adds a presumption that the accused committed the offence in respect of any drug whose possession he fails to account for satisfactorily. In Madan Lal v. State of H.P. (2003) the Supreme Court held that once possession is established, the person who says it was not conscious possession has to prove it, because how he came to be in possession is within his special knowledge. The real room for the defence is in the foundation: Baldev Singh held that the Section 54 presumption can be raised only after the prosecution has proved possession through a search that complied with Section 50. That is why, on a bail file before the Rajasthan High Court at Jodhpur, the seizure memo and the search record are read before anything else.

Bail under Section 37: Section 37(1)(b) applies to offences under Sections 19, 24 and 27A and to every offence involving a commercial quantity. The Public Prosecutor must be given an opportunity to oppose the application, and where he opposes it the court must be satisfied that (i) there are reasonable grounds for believing the accused is not guilty of the offence, and (ii) he is not likely to commit any offence while on bail. Section 37(2) makes these limits additional to the ordinary limits on bail. For small or intermediate quantity, unless the charge is under Section 19, 24 or 27A, Section 37 does not apply and the ordinary bail standards govern.

Sampling and chemical analysis: Section 52A(2) requires the seized drug to be inventoried and representative samples to be drawn in the presence of a Magistrate, who certifies the inventory, the photographs and the list of samples; Section 52A(4) makes those certified documents primary evidence at the trial. In Union of India v. Mohanlal (2016) the Supreme Court held that the drawing of samples has to be in the presence and under the supervision of the Magistrate. In Mangilal v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2023) it described Section 52A as a mandatory rule of evidence: without the Magistrate's certification, the inventory, photographs and list of samples do not constitute primary evidence. Improper sampling, or a gap in the chain of custody from seizure to the Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL), remains one of the strongest grounds on which the prosecution's evidence is tested.

Because Section 37 makes bail in commercial-quantity matters so much harder to obtain, the timing and forum of the application matter more here than in an ordinary case.
