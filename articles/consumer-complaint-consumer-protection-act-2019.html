How to File a Consumer Complaint Under the Consumer Protection Act 2019

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur April 2026

In short: A consumer complaint under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 goes to the District Commission where you live or where the cause of action arose. The forum is fixed by the consideration you paid, not by the compensation you claim. The limit is two years from the cause of action. Filing is in writing or on e-Daakhil.

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (Act 35 of 2019) was published in the Gazette on 9 August 2019, but its working provisions were only brought into force on 20 July 2020 by S.O. 2351(E) dated 15 July 2020. That gap matters more than it looks, because a great deal of what is written about the Act describes the Bill rather than the notified statute. What follows is read off the provisions as they stand, with the sub-sections named, because almost every consumer complaint we see returned at the filing counter is returned for a reason that sits in a sub-section somebody skipped.

Who counts as a consumer, and who does not

Section 2(7) defines a consumer in two limbs: one who buys goods for a consideration paid, promised, part-paid or under deferred payment, and one who hires or avails of a service on the same terms. Both limbs exclude a person who takes the goods for resale or for any commercial purpose. The Explanation to the clause then does two things that decide a surprising number of cases. Clause (a) says "commercial purpose" does not include goods bought and used by a person exclusively for earning his livelihood by self-employment. Clause (b) says "buys any goods" and "hires or avails any services" include offline or online transactions through electronic means, teleshopping, direct selling and multi-level marketing.

So a Jodhpur tailor who buys one industrial sewing machine and works it himself is a consumer. A unit that buys six and employs eight people to run them is usually not, and the word doing the work there is "exclusively". The distinction is worth settling before the complaint is drafted, because an objection on commercial purpose is the first thing a well-advised opposite party raises, and it goes to maintainability rather than merits.

Two more definitions earn their keep. Section 2(42) defines service very widely, naming banking, financing, insurance, transport, electricity, telecom, housing construction and the purveying of news, but it expressly excludes service rendered free of charge and service under a contract of personal service. Section 2(11) defines deficiency to include not only a fault or inadequacy in performance but also "deliberate withholding of relevant information" from the consumer. That second limb is the pleading that insurers and builders answer least comfortably.

Which Commission, and the test most pages get wrong

This is the point on which the largest number of complaints are filed in the wrong place. Section 34(1) gives the District Commission jurisdiction "where the value of the goods or services paid as consideration" does not exceed the prescribed figure. The consideration paid is the whole test. The compensation claimed does not enter it. The 1986 Act had used a combined measure of value plus compensation, and that older formula is still repeated on a great many websites, which is how a complaint that belonged at Jodhpur ends up carried to the State Commission.

As enacted, Section 34(1) set one crore rupees for the District Commission, Section 47(1)(a)(i) set above one crore up to ten crore for the State Commission, and Section 58(1)(a)(i) set above ten crore for the National Commission. Each of those three provisions carries a proviso letting the Central Government prescribe some other value. That power has been used. The Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction of the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission) Rules, 2021, notified on 30 December 2021, brought the figures down to fifty lakh rupees for the District Commission, above fifty lakh and up to two crore for the State Commission, and above two crore for the National Commission. Because the figures sit in rules and not in the section, the slab has to be checked against the rules in force on the day of filing.

Work it through. A buyer pays forty-five lakh rupees for a flat, possession is three years late, and he claims twenty lakh as compensation and interest. The consideration paid is forty-five lakh. The complaint lies before the District Commission. On the old combined test the sixty-five lakh total would have pushed the same case to the State Commission at Jaipur, with the travel and the delay that follows. Getting this right is not a technicality; it is the difference between a file heard in the district and a file heard three hundred kilometres away.

One limited jurisdiction does not come down to the district at all. A complaint against an unfair contract, as defined in Section 2(46), lies only before the State Commission under Section 47(1)(a)(ii) or the National Commission under Section 58(1)(a)(ii). The District Commission has no such power. Section 2(46) lists six kinds of term that qualify, including a manifestly excessive security deposit, a penalty wholly disproportionate to the loss, a refusal to accept early repayment of a debt, and unilateral termination without reasonable cause. Section 49(2) then lets the State Commission declare such a term null and void. Builder and loan agreements are full of clauses of exactly this description, and a complainant who wants one struck down has to choose his forum accordingly.

Filing at Jodhpur: the territorial limb that is new

Section 34(2) gives four territorial options. Clause (a) is the opposite party's residence, business, branch office or place of work. Clause (b) covers the case of several opposite parties where only one of them is there, and it works only with the Commission's permission. Clause (c) is where the cause of action arose, wholly or in part. Clause (d), the one that changed practice, is simply "the complainant resides or personally works for gain".

Clause (d) is why a Jodhpur consumer no longer has to follow the seller. A cheated online purchase from a Bengaluru marketplace, an insurance repudiation issued from Mumbai, a Gurugram builder's delayed flat: all of them can be taken to the District Commission here, on the strength of the complainant's own residence. Before 20 July 2020 that was not so, and the cost of the journey quietly defeated small claims. If you are weighing where to file, that is worth sitting down over with an advocate in Jodhpur and the papers you should bring to a first meeting, because the choice of limb also decides whether permission under clause (b) has to be asked for at all.

Who may file, and the two-year clock

Section 35(1) lists who may complain. Clause (a) is the consumer himself, including one who alleges an unfair trade practice. Clause (b) allows a recognised consumer association to file "whether the consumer is a member of such association or not", which is a real route for a claim too small to be worth one person's time. Clause (c) permits one or more consumers to file for all of them where numerous consumers have the same interest, with the Commission's permission, and that is the Act's class action. Clause (d) allows the Central Government, a State Government or the Central Authority to complain. The proviso to Section 35(1) permits electronic filing in the prescribed manner, which is the statutory basis of the e-Daakhil portal, and Section 35(2) requires the prescribed fee, payable in electronic form as well.

No advocate is required. A consumer may appear in person, and for a defective appliance or a railway refund that is often the sensible course.

Section 69(1) is strict: no Commission "shall admit" a complaint unless it is filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action arose. Section 69(2) allows a later complaint on sufficient cause, but its proviso adds a requirement that gets forgotten: the Commission must record its reasons for condoning the delay. Where reasons are not recorded the condonation itself becomes a ground of attack in appeal. So a delay application should be pleaded with dates, not with adjectives.

What happens after admission

Mediation is not offered when the complaint is received. Section 37(1) places it "at the first hearing of the complaint after its admission, or at any later stage", and only where the Commission sees elements of a settlement. The parties are then directed to give written consent within five days, and under Section 37(2) the Commission refers the matter within five days of that consent. Section 74 requires a consumer mediation cell attached to each District and State Commission. If a settlement is reached, Section 80 has it reduced to writing and signed, Section 81(1) requires an order recording it within seven days, and Section 81(2) lets a partly settled dispute proceed on the rest. There is a consequence worth knowing before consenting: the third proviso to Section 41 bars any appeal from an order passed under Section 81(1). A mediated settlement is final.

Where the matter proceeds, Section 38(2)(a) gives the Commission twenty-one days from admission to send a copy of the complaint to the opposite party, who then has thirty days to file his version, extendable by not more than fifteen. For an alleged defect in goods that cannot be judged without testing, clauses (c) to (f) route a sealed sample to an appropriate laboratory, which reports within forty-five days, with the fee deposited by the complainant and the report served on the opposite party. Section 38(7) sets the endeavour at three months from receipt of notice by the opposite party, or five months where testing is needed, and requires reasons in writing for every adjournment and for any disposal beyond that period.

The orders a Commission can actually pass

Section 39(1) runs from clause (a) to clause (n), and it is wider than most complainants ask for. Removal of the defect, replacement with goods free from defect, return of the price with interest, compensation for loss or injury caused by negligence, compensation in a product liability action, removal of a deficiency in service, discontinuance of an unfair or restrictive trade practice, withdrawal of hazardous goods, and costs. The proviso to clause (d) gives the power to award punitive damages. Clause (k) deals with loss suffered by a large number of consumers who cannot conveniently be identified, and its proviso fixes a floor of twenty-five per cent of the value of the defective goods or service supplied. Clauses (l) and (n) allow a corrective advertisement at the advertiser's cost and a cease-and-desist against a misleading one. Relief not claimed is routinely not granted, so the prayer clause should track these clauses by letter.

Product liability under Chapter VI

Chapter VI, Sections 82 to 87, is new law and is still underused. Section 83 allows a product liability action against a product manufacturer, a product service provider or a product seller for harm caused by a defective product. Section 84(1) makes a manufacturer liable on any of five grounds: a manufacturing defect, a defective design, a deviation from manufacturing specifications, non-conformity with an express warranty, or a failure to carry adequate instructions or warnings. Section 84(2) then removes the usual defence, because the manufacturer is liable on an express warranty "even if he proves that he was not negligent or fraudulent". Section 85 covers a faulty or deficient service, an act or omission or negligence, conscious withholding of information, or non-conformity with warranty. Section 86 reaches a seller who is not the manufacturer, and clause (d) catches him where the manufacturer is unknown or cannot be served, which is the practical answer to an imported product with no traceable maker. Section 87 sets out the exceptions, including misuse, alteration or modification at the time of harm, and a danger that is obvious or commonly known.

Appeals, and why the first stage decides the case

Section 41 gives an appeal to the State Commission on facts or law within forty-five days, with delay condonable on sufficient cause. Its second proviso is the one that bites: a person required to pay money under the order cannot appeal unless he first deposits fifty per cent of that amount. Section 51(1) gives a further appeal to the National Commission within thirty days, again on a fifty per cent deposit. Section 51(2) then narrows the road considerably, because an appeal from an order passed by a State Commission in appeal lies only where the National Commission is satisfied that a substantial question of law is involved, and Section 51(3) requires the memorandum to state that question precisely.

Read together, that is why the District Commission stage is where a consumer case is won or lost. Once the Rajasthan State Commission at Jaipur has decided an appeal under Section 47(1)(a)(iii), a second appeal is not available as of right, and what is left is the High Court's supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227, which at Jodhpur means the Principal Seat of the Rajasthan High Court. That jurisdiction corrects jurisdictional error. It does not re-appreciate evidence that was never led.

One transitional point still comes up for older files. In Neena Aneja v. Jai Prakash Associates Ltd., decided on 16 March 2021, the Supreme Court held that proceedings instituted before the Act of 2019 commenced on 20 July 2020 continue before the fora corresponding to those under the 1986 Act and are not to be transferred in terms of the new pecuniary limits. A complaint filed in 2019 therefore stays where it was filed, whatever the 2021 Rules now say.

Enforcement, where most complainants give up

An order is not the end of the work. Section 71 requires every order of a District, State or National Commission to be enforced by it "in the same manner as if it were a decree made by a Court in a suit before it", with Order XXI of the First Schedule to the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 applying as far as may be. So attachment and sale are available, through the Commission itself and not through a separate suit.

Section 72 adds teeth that are rarely invoked. Failure to comply with an order is punishable with imprisonment of not less than one month and up to three years, or a fine of not less than twenty-five thousand rupees and up to one lakh, or both. Section 72(2) gives the Commission the powers of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class to try that offence. A builder or an insurer who treats an award as a negotiating position is exposed under this section, and an application under it is usually more effective than a fresh round of correspondence.

Questions we are asked

Which Commission do I file in, and does my compensation claim change that?

No. Section 34(1) measures jurisdiction by the value of the goods or services paid as consideration, and the compensation claimed is left out of the calculation. Under the 2021 Rules the District Commission takes consideration up to fifty lakh rupees, the State Commission above fifty lakh and up to two crore, and the National Commission above two crore. The one exception is a complaint against an unfair contract under Section 2(46), which lies only before the State Commission under Section 47(1)(a)(ii) or the National Commission under Section 58(1)(a)(ii).

Can I file at Jodhpur against a company that has no office here?

Yes, in most cases. Section 34(2)(d) founds jurisdiction on the place where the complainant resides or personally works for gain, and Section 34(2)(c) on the place where the cause of action arose wholly or in part. Either limb is enough to bring an online purchase, a repudiated insurance claim or a delayed flat before the District Commission here. Where there are several opposite parties and you rely on the residence of only one of them, Section 34(2)(b) requires the Commission's permission.

How late is too late, and can delay be excused?

Section 69(1) bars admission of a complaint filed more than two years after the cause of action arose. Section 69(2) allows a later complaint where sufficient cause is shown, but its proviso requires the Commission to record reasons for condoning the delay. Plead the delay with dates and documents. A bare statement that the complainant was pursuing the company is weak, and an order condoning delay without recorded reasons is itself open to challenge in appeal.

Do I need an advocate, and what does filing on e-Daakhil involve?

An advocate is not required. Section 35(1) lets the consumer complain himself, and the proviso to that sub-section permits electronic filing, which is what the e-Daakhil portal at edaakhil.nic.in implements; Section 35(2) requires the prescribed fee, payable electronically. A recognised consumer association may also file under Section 35(1)(b) whether or not you are its member, and numerous consumers with the same interest may file jointly under Section 35(1)(c) with permission. Where the claim turns on an unfair contract term, a product liability action under Chapter VI, or a fifty per cent pre-deposit on appeal, the drafting is worth professional help.

I have an order in my favour and the company is ignoring it. What now?

Two remedies run together. Section 71 makes the order executable by the Commission itself as if it were a civil court decree, with Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 applying, so attachment is available without a fresh suit. Section 72 makes non-compliance a punishable offence, with imprisonment of one month to three years or a fine of twenty-five thousand to one lakh rupees, and Section 72(2) gives the Commission the powers of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class to try it.
