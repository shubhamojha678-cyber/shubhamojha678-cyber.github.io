Arbitration vs. Litigation: Choosing the Right Dispute Resolution Path

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur April 2026

In short: Arbitration is open to you only where there is a written arbitration agreement under Section 7 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. It is usually quicker, because Section 23(4) and Section 29A put the pleadings and the award on fixed clocks. Courts stay necessary for writ relief, for non-signatories and for insolvency. A Section 34 challenge does not by itself stop enforcement.

Most contracts we are shown contain an arbitration clause that nobody read before signing. It names a venue, sometimes an arbitrator, occasionally an institution that no longer exists. The clause matters far more than the parties expect, because it decides which building the dispute goes to, how long it will take, and how little a court may later interfere. What follows is read off the Act section by section, with the sub-sections named, because the choice between arbitration and a suit turns on sub-sections and not on general preference.

The only question that decides it: Section 7

Section 7(1) defines an arbitration agreement as an agreement to submit present or future disputes in respect of a defined legal relationship to arbitration. Section 7(3) requires it to be in writing. Section 7(4) then explains what writing means, and it is wider than a signed contract: a document signed by the parties, or an exchange of letters, telex, telegrams or other means of telecommunication including communication through electronic means which provide a record of the agreement, or an exchange of statements of claim and defence in which one party alleges the agreement and the other does not deny it. Section 7(5) covers incorporation by reference, where a written contract refers to another document containing an arbitration clause in terms that make the clause part of the contract.

That last limb saves a good number of purchase-order disputes. A buyer who placed orders against printed terms carrying an arbitration clause is usually bound by it, even though he never signed those terms separately. Conversely, an oral understanding to arbitrate is worth nothing.

If a suit is filed in the teeth of such an agreement, Section 8(1) is the answer, and it has a deadline inside it. The application to refer must be made not later than the date of submitting the first statement on the substance of the dispute. File a written statement on the merits first and the right is gone. Section 8(2) requires the original arbitration agreement or a duly certified copy to accompany the application, with a proviso for the case where the other side is holding the original: file a copy and ask the court to call for the original. The judicial authority must refer the parties unless it finds that prima facie no valid arbitration agreement exists.

The two clocks: Section 23(4) and Section 29A

This is where most published summaries go wrong, including the earlier version of this note. The twelve-month period in Section 29A(1) does not run from the arbitrator's appointment. As it reads after the 2019 amendment, the award in a matter other than an international commercial arbitration must be made within twelve months from the date of completion of pleadings under sub-section (4) of Section 23. Section 23(4), inserted by the same amendment, requires the statement of claim and defence to be completed within six months from the date on which the arbitrator or all the arbitrators received notice in writing of their appointment.

So the honest outside figure for a domestic arbitration is eighteen months, not twelve. Section 29A(3) allows the parties by consent to extend the award period by a further six months. After that, Section 29A(4) terminates the mandate of the arbitrators unless the Court has extended the period, before or after expiry, and Section 29A(5) permits extension only for sufficient cause. The proviso to Section 29A(4) carries a sanction that is seldom asked for: where the Court finds the delay attributable to the tribunal, it may reduce the arbitrators' fees by up to five per cent for each month of delay, after hearing the arbitrator.

An international commercial arbitration is outside the hard limit. The proviso to Section 29A(1) only says the award may be made as expeditiously as possible, with an endeavour to dispose of the matter within twelve months of the completion of pleadings. Parties sometimes assume the strict clock applies to a contract with a foreign party. It does not.

Which door you knock on at Jodhpur

Section 2(1)(e)(i) is the provision to read before filing anything. For an arbitration other than an international commercial arbitration, "Court" means the principal Civil Court of original jurisdiction in a district, including a High Court exercising ordinary original civil jurisdiction, but not a court inferior to that principal Civil Court and not a Court of Small Causes. The Rajasthan High Court has no ordinary original civil jurisdiction. The practical consequence is one that surprises new litigants here: an application under Section 9 for an interim measure, and a petition under Section 34 to set aside a domestic award, are presented before the District Judge at Jodhpur, not before the High Court bench.

Appointment is the exception. Section 11(4) and Section 11(6), as they now read, place the appointment in a domestic arbitration with the High Court on an application by a party, so that application is filed at the Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur. The triggers are specific. Section 11(4)(a) applies where a party fails to appoint within thirty days of receiving a request to do so. Section 11(4)(b) applies where the two appointed arbitrators fail to agree on the presiding arbitrator within thirty days of their own appointment. Section 11(5) applies to a sole arbitrator where the parties do not agree within thirty days of a request. Section 11(3A) allows the Supreme Court and the High Courts to designate graded arbitral institutions, with a proviso permitting the Chief Justice of the High Court to maintain a panel of arbitrators where no graded institution is available in that jurisdiction.

Appeals follow Section 37, which is a closed list and says so. Section 37(1) allows an appeal only from an order refusing to refer under Section 8, granting or refusing a measure under Section 9, or setting aside or refusing to set aside an award under Section 34, and it lies to the court authorised to hear appeals from original decrees of the court that passed the order. Section 37(2) adds two appeals from the tribunal itself: from an order accepting a plea under Section 16(2) or Section 16(3), and from an order granting or refusing an interim measure under Section 17. Note the asymmetry. If the tribunal accepts a jurisdiction objection, the other side may appeal at once. If it rejects one, there is no appeal, and the point waits for Section 34. Section 37(3) bars a second appeal.

Section 42 then fixes everything in place. Once any application under Part I has been made in a Court, that Court alone has jurisdiction over the arbitral proceedings and all subsequent applications arising out of that agreement. A party who rushes a Section 9 application to a convenient court has chosen the forum for the Section 34 petition as well. Where a commercial dispute of the Specified Value is involved, Section 10(3) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 routes applications that would ordinarily lie before the principal civil court to the Commercial Court exercising territorial jurisdiction, and Section 13(1) gives an appeal to the Commercial Appellate Division of the High Court within sixty days, its proviso expressly carrying orders under Section 37 of the Arbitration Act. If you are weighing which of these forums your contract actually points to, it is worth sitting down with advocates in Jodhpur and which of these forums hears a contract dispute before the first application is filed rather than after.

Who cannot be your arbitrator: Section 12(5) and the Seventh Schedule

Section 12(5) begins with the words "notwithstanding any prior agreement to the contrary", and that is the whole of its force. Any person whose relationship with the parties, with counsel, or with the subject-matter falls in a category listed in the Seventh Schedule is ineligible to be appointed. Entry 1 of that Schedule covers an arbitrator who is an employee, consultant or advisor of a party, or who has any other past or present business relationship with a party. Entry 4 covers a lawyer in the same firm that is representing a party.

Older contracts, and government and public-sector contracts in particular, routinely name a serving officer of one party as the arbitrator. Such a clause cannot be enforced today. The only escape is in the proviso: the parties may waive Section 12(5) by an express agreement in writing, and only after disputes have arisen between them. A waiver buried in the original contract is no waiver at all. Separately, Section 13(2) gives a party fifteen days from becoming aware of the tribunal's constitution, or of a circumstance under Section 12(3), to raise a challenge, so an objection kept in the pocket for later is usually lost.

Setting aside an award: Section 34, and what it is not

Section 34(1) is restrictive by design. Recourse to a Court against an award may be had only by an application to set it aside in accordance with sub-sections (2) and (3). There is no appeal on the merits.

The 2019 amendment made a change to Section 34(2)(a) that decides how these petitions are now conducted. The words "furnishes proof that" were substituted by "establishes on the basis of the record of the arbitral tribunal that". The challenge is therefore argued on the tribunal's record. Fresh affidavits and documents are not the route, and a petition drafted as though it were a first appeal invites dismissal.

The grounds in Section 34(2)(a) are incapacity of a party, invalidity of the arbitration agreement, want of proper notice of the appointment or of the proceedings or inability otherwise to present one's case, an award dealing with matters beyond the scope of the submission, and a tribunal or procedure not in accordance with the parties' agreement. The proviso to clause (iv) permits severance: where the matters properly submitted can be separated, only the excess part is set aside. Section 34(2)(b) adds two grounds the Court may find for itself, non-arbitrability of the subject-matter and conflict with the public policy of India. Explanation 1 confines public policy to three limbs: an award induced or affected by fraud or corruption or in violation of Section 75 or Section 81, contravention of the fundamental policy of Indian law, and conflict with the most basic notions of morality or justice. Explanation 2 shuts the door that used to be pushed open through this ground, by providing that the fundamental-policy test shall not entail a review on the merits.

Section 34(2A) gives domestic awards one further ground, patent illegality appearing on the face of the award. Its proviso is as important as the ground: an award shall not be set aside merely for an erroneous application of the law or by re-appreciation of evidence. Patent illegality is not a disagreement with the arbitrator's reading of the contract.

The limitation is unforgiving. Section 34(3) allows three months from receipt of the award, or from disposal of a Section 33 request for correction or interpretation, extendable by thirty days for sufficient cause "but not thereafter". There is no further discretion. Section 34(5) requires a prior notice to the other party and an affidavit endorsing that compliance, and Section 34(6) directs disposal within one year of service of that notice.

An award is not self-executing: Section 36

Section 36(1) makes an award enforceable under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in the same manner as if it were a decree of the court, once the time for a Section 34 application has expired. The structure of Section 36(2) is the part award-holders and award-debtors both tend to get wrong. Filing a Section 34 petition does not by itself render the award unenforceable. A stay must be applied for separately, and granted, with reasons recorded in writing under Section 36(3).

The first proviso to Section 36(3) requires the Court, in the case of a money award, to have due regard to the provisions for stay of a money decree under the Code. In practice that means a deposit or security is the usual price of a stay. The second proviso, inserted in 2021 with retrospective effect from 23 October 2015, goes the other way: where the Court is satisfied that a prima facie case is made out that the arbitration agreement or the contract underlying the award, or the making of the award itself, was induced or effected by fraud or corruption, it shall stay the award unconditionally pending disposal of the Section 34 challenge.

Where arbitration does not reach

Arbitration is a creature of the agreement, which is also its limit. A claim against the State or a statutory authority that needs writ relief under Article 226 cannot be taken to a tribunal. A dispute that must bind persons who never signed the agreement will not be fully resolved by an award, because Section 35 makes an award final and binding on the parties and on persons claiming under them, and no further. Insolvency and winding-up are statutory jurisdictions. Consumer grievances lie before the statutory Commissions. And where the opposing party is likely to resist at every stage, the time spent on an award, a Section 34 petition and then execution can exceed the time a well-prosecuted suit would have taken.

Arbitration earns its place in three settings. Technical disputes, in construction and supply contracts, where an arbitrator who understands the trade is worth more than a generalist. Cross-border contracts, where neither side wants the other's home court. And matters where confidentiality has real commercial value, Section 42A now requiring the arbitrator, the institution and the parties to maintain confidentiality of the proceedings subject to disclosure for enforcement.

A Jodhpur supplier's faster road: Section 18 of the MSMED Act

If you supply goods or services from a registered micro or small enterprise, there is a statutory route that is quicker than both arbitration under a contract and a civil suit, and most suppliers here have never used it. The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006 does the work.

Section 15 obliges the buyer to pay on the agreed date, and its proviso caps the agreed period: in no case shall the period agreed in writing exceed forty-five days from the day of acceptance or deemed acceptance. Section 16 then makes a defaulting buyer liable, notwithstanding any agreement or any other law, to compound interest with monthly rests at three times the bank rate notified by the Reserve Bank, running from the appointed day or from the day after the agreed date.

Section 18(1) lets any party to such a dispute refer it to the Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Council, notwithstanding anything in any other law in force. The Council first conducts conciliation, or refers it for conciliation, and Section 18(2) applies Sections 65 to 81 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 to that stage. If conciliation terminates without settlement, Section 18(3) requires the Council itself to take up the dispute as arbitration, or to refer it to an alternative dispute resolution centre, and the 1996 Act then applies as if there were an arbitration agreement under Section 7. No arbitration clause in the supply contract is needed at all.

Two sub-sections make this route genuinely valuable to a Jodhpur handicraft, stone or textile supplier. Section 18(4) gives the Council where the supplier is located jurisdiction over a buyer located anywhere in India. Section 18(5) requires every reference to be decided within ninety days. And Section 19 is the teeth: no application to set aside the Council's award or order shall be entertained by any court unless the appellant, not being the supplier, has deposited seventy-five per cent of the awarded amount, with a proviso allowing the court to release a reasonable part of that deposit to the supplier while the challenge is pending.

If the route is a commercial suit: Section 12A and the District Legal Services Authority

Where there is no arbitration agreement and the dispute is a commercial dispute of the Specified Value, the suit cannot simply be filed. Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 requires a plaintiff in "a suit, which does not contemplate any urgent interim relief" to exhaust pre-institution mediation first. In Patil Automation Private Limited v. Rakheja Engineers Private Limited, 2022 SCC OnLine SC 1028, decided on 17 August 2022, the Supreme Court held the requirement mandatory, held that a plaint filed in breach is to be rejected under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code, and made clear at paragraphs 75 and 76 that the Commercial Division may reject suo motu without waiting for an application. Paragraph 92 fixed 20 August 2022 as the date from which the declaration operates. A fresh plaint after compliance remains open under Order VII Rule 13, but the court fee already spent is lost.

The machinery is in the Commercial Courts (Pre-Institution Mediation and Settlement) Rules, 2018, framed under Section 21A(2) read with Section 12A(1). Rule 3(1) requires an application in Form-1 to the Authority, and Rule 10 identifies that Authority as the District Legal Services Authority, which at Jodhpur means the DLSA in the district court complex. Rule 3(2) requires notice to the opposite party to appear and consent within ten days. Rule 3(4) and Rule 3(6) provide that where the opposite party does not respond, refuses to participate, or fails to appear on the alternate date, the mediation is treated as a non-starter and a report issues in Form-3. That report is what the plaint must be accompanied by. Rule 3(8) caps the process at three months, extendable by two months with the consent of both sides. An overseas buyer in a dispute with an Indian supplier should read this alongside our separate note on what an overseas buyer can do when a Jodhpur exporter has taken payment and not shipped, because the non-starter report is usually the realistic outcome and it is worth obtaining early.

A foreign award against an Indian party

Part II of the Act handles this and the forum changes. Section 44 defines a foreign award as one made in pursuance of a written arbitration agreement to which the Convention in the First Schedule applies, in a territory notified by the Central Government as reciprocating. The Explanation to Section 47 provides that in Part II "Court" means the High Court. So a New York Convention award against a party in this region is taken to the Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur, and not to the District Judge.

Section 47(1) requires the applicant to produce the original award or a duly authenticated copy, the original arbitration agreement or a certified copy, and evidence that the award is a foreign award. Section 47(2) requires a certified English translation where the papers are in a foreign language, which is a step foreign claimants regularly overlook. Section 48(1) limits refusal of enforcement to grounds the resisting party must prove, parallel to Section 34 but narrower. Once the Court is satisfied, Section 49 deems the foreign award to be a decree of that Court, and Section 50(2) bars a second appeal.

Limitation, which is where contracts die quietly

Section 43(1) applies the Limitation Act, 1963 to arbitrations as it applies to proceedings in court. There is no longer period because the forum is private. Section 43(2) fixes the date of commencement by reference to Section 21, and Section 21 is precise: unless otherwise agreed, arbitral proceedings in respect of a particular dispute commence on the date on which the request for that dispute to be referred to arbitration is received by the respondent.

Receipt, not despatch. A notice invoking arbitration that is sent to a stale address and never received does not stop the clock, and we have seen three-year claims lost on exactly that. Keep proof of service. Section 43(3) allows the Court to extend a contractual time-bar on a claim where undue hardship would otherwise be caused. Section 43(4) excludes, in computing limitation afresh, the period between commencement of the arbitration and the date of an order setting the award aside. Section 31A, finally, gives the Court or the tribunal discretion on costs notwithstanding the Code, covering arbitrators' and witnesses' fees and expenses, legal fees and expenses, institutional administration fees and other expenses of the proceedings.

Questions we are asked

Our contract says disputes go to arbitration. Can I still file a civil suit?

You can present it, but it will not survive an objection. Section 8(1) obliges the judicial authority to refer the parties to arbitration if a party applies, unless it finds that prima facie no valid arbitration agreement exists. The application must come not later than the date of submitting the first statement on the substance of the dispute. The same deadline cuts the other way: if the other side files a written statement on the merits without applying under Section 8, the objection is generally treated as given up.

How long does an arbitration actually take under the 1996 Act?

Eighteen months is the statutory outside figure for a domestic arbitration, not twelve. Section 23(4) allows six months from notice of appointment for pleadings to be completed, and Section 29A(1) then allows twelve months from completion of pleadings for the award. Section 29A(3) permits a further six months by consent, after which only the Court may extend under Section 29A(4) and (5). An international commercial arbitration has no hard limit, only the endeavour stated in the proviso to Section 29A(1).

The award is against me. Does filing under Section 34 stop recovery?

No. Section 36(2) says in terms that filing a Section 34 application does not by itself render the award unenforceable. You must apply separately for a stay, and Section 36(3) requires reasons in writing where one is granted. For a money award the first proviso directs the Court to have regard to the rules for staying a money decree under the Code, so expect a condition. The exception is the second proviso, which mandates an unconditional stay where a prima facie case of fraud or corruption in the contract or in the making of the award is made out. Remember Section 34(3): three months, with thirty days more for sufficient cause and not a day beyond.

Our contract names the other side's director as the arbitrator. Is that clause valid?

Not as it stands. Section 12(5) operates notwithstanding any prior agreement to the contrary, and Entry 1 of the Seventh Schedule makes a person who is an employee, consultant or advisor of a party, or who has any other past or present business relationship with a party, ineligible for appointment. The only cure is an express written waiver executed after the dispute has arisen. Otherwise the appointment route is an application to the High Court under Section 11.

I supply from Jodhpur and a buyer outside Rajasthan has not paid. Is arbitration my best route?

If you are a registered micro or small enterprise, look at Section 18 of the MSMED Act, 2006 before you look at your contract. A reference to the Facilitation Council needs no arbitration clause, Section 18(4) gives the Council where you are located jurisdiction over a buyer anywhere in India, Section 18(5) sets a ninety-day outer limit, and Section 19 requires a buyer challenging the award to deposit seventy-five per cent of it first. Section 16 adds compound interest with monthly rests at three times the Reserve Bank's notified bank rate. Bring the purchase orders, the invoices, the proof of delivery or acceptance, and your registration particulars.
