When Can You File a Writ Petition? Understanding Articles 226 & 227 of the Constitution

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur April 2026
Papers in hand? A photo of the notice, order or petition can be sent on WhatsApp to +91 70230 51275, or call the same number (Mon–Sat, 12 noon – 10 PM).

A writ petition under Article 226 asks a High Court to correct an act of a public authority; Article 227 asks it to keep a subordinate court or tribunal inside its jurisdiction. At Jodhpur the petition goes to the Bench for the district where the cause of action arose, and the Court asks first what other remedy the statute gives.

Article 226 of the Constitution of India vests in every High Court the power to issue directions, orders, or writs including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari to any person or authority, including the government. This jurisdiction is wider than the Supreme Court's Article 32 jurisdiction in two respects: it covers violations of legal rights (not just fundamental rights), and it extends to all territory within the High Court's jurisdiction. Article 227 gives the High Court supervisory jurisdiction over all courts and tribunals within its territory.

Types of writs: Habeas corpus ("you shall have the body") is issued to secure release of a person detained unlawfully — it is the most urgent writ and must be heard immediately. Mandamus ("we command") directs a public authority to perform a duty imposed by law. Certiorari is issued to quash a decision of a lower court, tribunal, or authority that acted without jurisdiction, in excess of jurisdiction, or in violation of natural justice principles. Prohibition prevents a lower court or tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction. Quo warranto calls upon a person to show by what authority they hold a public office.

The alternative remedy bar: A significant limitation on writ jurisdiction is the doctrine of alternative remedy. High Courts generally decline to exercise Article 226 jurisdiction where the petitioner has an adequate, efficacious, and equally convenient alternative remedy available. In Whirlpool Corporation v. Registrar of Trade Marks (1998), the Supreme Court laid down exceptions: where the action of the authority is wholly without jurisdiction; where there is a violation of principles of natural justice; where the impugned order or statute is unconstitutional; or where the vires of the statute is under challenge.

Article 227 — supervisory jurisdiction: Unlike Article 226, Article 227 cannot be used to substitute the High Court's judgment for that of the lower court on questions of fact. It is limited to ensuring that inferior courts remain within the bounds of their jurisdiction. In Shalini Shyam Shetty v. Rajendra Shankar Patil (2010), the Supreme Court held that Article 227 jurisdiction is not appellate and should be sparingly used to correct jurisdictional errors only.

Practice before the Rajasthan High Court: Writ petitions at the Rajasthan High Court (Jodhpur Bench) are filed in the Registry with a vakalatnama, the petition, an affidavit in support, and annexures. Urgent matters can be mentioned before the roster Bench for early listing. Courts routinely issue stay orders on impugned decisions pending hearing, making Article 226 an effective tool for interim relief in service, revenue, and regulatory matters.

Where a tribunal stands in the way: A government servant cannot come straight to the High Court where a service tribunal has jurisdiction — under L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) the Central Administrative Tribunal is approached first, and its order is then challenged before a Division Bench under Article 226/227. The forums, limitation periods and departmental steps that decide this are set out in सरकारी कर्मचारी के सेवा विवाद — CAT, अधिकरण या High Court (Hindi). The same ground in English, including the CCA Rules inquiry a major penalty has to survive and where pension is in issue, is covered in service law matters: challenging government orders through writ petitions.

Where the bar bites hardest, bank recovery: Once a bank’s dues are in issue the Whirlpool exceptions are read narrowly, and United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon (Supreme Court, 2010) applies the rule with greater rigour: a borrower aggrieved by a District Magistrate’s possession order takes the objection to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17(1) of the SARFAESI Act, 2002 within forty-five days of the measure, as worked out in the Magistrate’s possession order under Section 14 and the limits on it, and from the Tribunal’s order the way onward is the statutory appeal described in the Section 18 appeal and its pre-deposit, not a petition under Article 226.

The two-week clock in Article 226(3): clause (3) matters after the stay, not before it. It applies only where the interim order was passed without copies of the petition and of the documents supporting the plea for interim relief having been furnished to the opposite party, and without that party being heard. If such a party then applies for vacation and furnishes a copy of the application to the party in whose favour the order was made or to that party’s counsel, the application must be disposed of within two weeks of the date it is received or the date the copy is so furnished, whichever is later — or, where the Court is closed on the last day, by the next day on which it opens. If it is not disposed of, the interim order stands vacated on the expiry of that period. No order is needed. The clause does it by itself. So an ex parte stay taken on the first date at Jodhpur is worth what the service and the reply after it are worth. Clause (2) is the other one worth reading early: it lets the petition be presented here when the cause of action arises in this State wholly or in part, “notwithstanding that the seat of such Government or authority or the residence of such person is not within those territories”. What a High Court matter needs when it is brought to chamber is listed in the note on advocates in Jodhpur and what to bring for a High Court matter.

Filing a writ petition at the Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur

Which Bench. The cause-of-action test in clause (2), set out above, is what settles this, and it is worth settling before anything is drafted. The Rajasthan High Court sits at Jodhpur, its principal seat, and at Jaipur. Matters arising from the western districts — Jodhpur, Barmer, Jaisalmer, Pali, Sirohi and Jalore among them — are filed here; matters from the Jaipur side go to the Jaipur Bench. What decides it is ordinarily where the cause of action arose, or where the subordinate court or authority sits, and not where the petitioner happens to live: a Jodhpur resident whose revenue proceedings ran in an eastern tehsil may well find that the petition belongs at Jaipur. The reverse happens as often. The fuller forum map — which of the Jodhpur courts, tribunals and revenue authorities a matter belongs to in the first place — is in the note on which forum hears your matter at Jodhpur. A petition presented at the wrong Bench is not usually fatal. But it costs a listing and an objection. The time lost can turn into a delay point that was not there before.

What the petition has to show on its own face. Clause (1) is drafted around an authority: the writ issues “to any person or authority, including in appropriate cases, any Government”, and it issues both “for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III and for any other purpose”. Two things follow for the drafting. First, the respondent has to be the authority whose act or omission is complained of, named as the statute under which it acted names it. Not the department in the abstract. Second, the petitioner need not find a fundamental right. Article 14 (equality before the law), Article 16 (equality of opportunity in public employment) and Article 21 (life and personal liberty) are the three most often pleaded here, in service and in detention matters; but “any other purpose” takes in a legal right under a statute or a rule, which is what most revenue and service petitions actually rest on. The order or action challenged is annexed, and the prayer is framed against it — a petition that asks for a direction without putting the order on record leaves the Court nothing to quash.

The dates, and why they are asked for first. No period is prescribed for a petition under Article 226. Section 3(1) of the Limitation Act, 1963 dismisses a “suit instituted, appeal preferred, and application made after the prescribed period”, and the Schedule to that Act prescribes no period for a writ petition at all. The objection therefore does not arrive as a statutory bar. It arrives as delay. The point is taken at the first listing. A chronology carrying the date of the impugned order, the date it was served or otherwise came to the petitioner’s knowledge, the date of any representation made to the authority and the date of its reply or refusal answers that objection before it is put. Where the gap is long and the cause of it is real — a certified copy that took months to come, an appeal pursued before the wrong forum — the reason belongs in the petition itself, not in an explanation offered at the bar.

The alternative-remedy question comes at the first listing. The rule and the Whirlpool exceptions are set out above; what matters at the filing stage is that the petition should say which exception is relied on, and why the statutory remedy is not adequate in this particular matter. Where the statute carries its own forum — a Securitisation Application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal, a departmental appeal under the service rules, a revenue appeal upward to the Board of Revenue — that exception has to be pleaded. It is not assumed. The papers themselves are the ordinary High Court set: the petition, an affidavit in support, the vakalatnama, and the annexures in the order the petition cites them.

Article 226 or Article 227

The two articles are aimed at different targets. Article 226 is aimed at a person, an authority or a Government, and it issues a direction, order or writ against what that body did or failed to do. Article 227 is aimed at the courts: clause (1) gives every High Court “superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction”. The remainder of the article shows what kind of power that is. Clause (2) lets the High Court call for returns from those courts, make and issue general rules and prescribe forms regulating their practice and proceedings, and prescribe the forms in which their books, entries and accounts are kept; clause (3) lets it settle tables of fees for the sheriff, clerks and officers and for advocates and pleaders practising there, with the Governor’s previous approval. That is a supervisory and corrective power, administrative as much as judicial, which is why — as noted above — Shalini Shyam Shetty keeps it outside the appellate field.

Three textual differences worth knowing before the petition is labelled. Clause (4) of Article 227 withholds superintendence over any court or tribunal constituted by or under a law relating to the Armed Forces, so that route is closed wherever the forum is a military one, whatever the grievance may be. That is a hard limit. Article 227 also carries no counterpart to Article 226(3): the two-week provision described above, which vacates an ex parte interim order of its own force, is attached to petitions under clause (1) of Article 226, and an interim order made in a petition resting on superintendence alone does not fall away on that clock. And Article 226(4) states expressly that the High Court’s power “shall not be in derogation of the power conferred on the Supreme Court by clause (2) of article 32” — the two remedies stand side by side, with Article 32 confined to the enforcement of Part III rights and Article 226 reaching wider.

In practice the label matters less than the ground. A petition at Jodhpur against an order of a subordinate court or a tribunal is commonly headed under both articles, and the Court takes the ground as it finds it: an authority acting without jurisdiction, a breach of natural justice, an order resting on no material. The heading is not the argument. What it cannot do is convert a question of fact into a question of jurisdiction. There is also a third route, and it is not a writ petition at all: under Article 228, where a case pending in a court subordinate to the High Court involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, the determination of which is necessary for disposing of that case, the High Court withdraws the case and may either dispose of it itself or decide the question of law and return the case with a copy of its judgment, which the subordinate court then follows.

Questions we are asked about writ petitions

Which Bench of the Rajasthan High Court will hear my writ petition — Jodhpur or Jaipur?

Ordinarily the Bench for the territory in which the cause of action arose, wholly or in part, which is the test in Article 226(2) — not the Bench nearest the petitioner’s home. Matters from the western districts, among them Jodhpur, Barmer, Jaisalmer, Pali, Sirohi and Jalore, are filed at Jodhpur; matters from the Jaipur side at Jaipur. Where the subordinate court or the authority sits usually decides it.

Is there a time limit for filing a writ petition under Article 226?

No period is prescribed. Section 3(1) of the Limitation Act, 1963 dismisses a suit, appeal or application made after the prescribed period, and the Schedule to that Act fixes no period for a writ petition. The objection comes instead as delay, and it is taken at the first listing — so the petition should carry the date of the impugned order, the date of knowledge, and the dates of any representation and of its reply.

Can a writ petition be filed when the statute already provides an appeal or a tribunal?

It can be presented, but the High Court will ordinarily ask why the statutory remedy was not used first. The Whirlpool exceptions — want of jurisdiction, breach of natural justice, an unconstitutional order or statute, or a challenge to the vires — have to be pleaded in the petition itself. In bank recovery the rule is applied more strictly, and a Securitisation Application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17 of the SARFAESI Act, 2002 is the ordinary route.

What is the difference between Article 226 and Article 227?

Article 226 issues a direction, order or writ against a person, an authority or a Government, for the enforcement of a Part III right or “for any other purpose”. Article 227 is the High Court’s power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals within its territory, and it is corrective rather than appellate. Article 227 does not reach a court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the Armed Forces, and it carries no counterpart to the two-week vacation provision in Article 226(3).
