Divorce Matters in Jodhpur: Mutual Consent, Contested Petitions, and the Family Court

A marriage ends in Jodhpur in one of two ways on paper: a joint petition under Section 13-B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, or a contested petition under Section 13. This page sets out which court takes each, what the court must be shown, and when a matter reaches the Rajasthan High Court.

The Family Court at Jodhpur hears every divorce petition from the district, and most of the questions a family brings to the chamber at Ratanada are about sequence rather than law. Which petition. Which court. How many hearings. What happens if the other side does not come. This page answers those in order, and leaves the law of Section 13-B itself to the longer note on Section 13-B procedure, the two motions, and waiver of the cooling-off period. Which forum at Jodhpur takes the other matters a family may have, from maintenance to property, is on the page on which court at Jodhpur hears which matter.

Mutual consent under Section 13-B of the Hindu Marriage Act

Section 13-B(1) asks for three averments, and the petition is presented by both spouses together: that they have been living separately for one year or more, that they have not been able to live together, and that they have mutually agreed that the marriage should be dissolved. Living separately does not always mean separate roofs. Courts have accepted spouses under one roof who have ceased to live as husband and wife, but the petition then has to say so plainly and the date has to be fixed. That date is tested first. The first hearing, called the first motion, is where the Family Court records the statements of both parties. Section 13-B(2) then imposes a wait: the second motion is made not earlier than six months after presentation and not later than eighteen months after it. At the second motion the court hears both sides again, makes such inquiry as it thinks fit, and if satisfied that the marriage was solemnised and the averments are true, passes the decree with effect from that date.

Two practical points decide how long this actually takes at Jodhpur. The first is whether the terms are already written. A petition that annexes the settlement, covering permanent alimony, the return of articles, custody and visitation, and the withdrawal of any pending complaint, goes through the first motion in one hearing. One that leaves those terms to be agreed later does not. Settle the terms first. The second is attendance. Both parties must be present at both motions, in person or through a power of attorney holder where the court permits it, and a spouse abroad should settle that question before the petition is filed rather than at the second motion. The eighteen-month outer limit in Section 13-B(2) runs from presentation, so a petition that is left to drift lapses and has to be presented afresh.

Contested divorce under Section 13: grounds and what the Family Court actually examines

A contested petition is presented by one spouse on a ground in Section 13(1): adultery, cruelty, desertion for a continuous period of not less than two years immediately before the petition, conversion to another religion, incurable unsoundness of mind or a mental disorder of the kind the Explanation describes, renunciation of the world, or not having been heard of as alive for seven years. Section 13(1A) adds two grounds available to either spouse: no resumption of cohabitation for one year after a decree of judicial separation, and no restitution of conjugal rights for one year after a decree under Section 9. Section 13(2) gives a wife four further grounds of her own. Section 14 bars a petition within one year of the marriage unless the court grants leave on an application, and the Family Court at Jodhpur does ask why leave is sought. Leave is not automatic.

What the court examines is evidence of the ground, not the state of the marriage in general. Cruelty is the ground pleaded most often here, and it is proved through dates, letters, messages, medical papers and the witnesses who saw the conduct, not through adjectives in the petition. Adjectives prove nothing. Desertion needs the date it began and proof that it was without cause and without consent. Section 23(1) requires the court to be satisfied that the petitioner is not taking advantage of his or her own wrong, that there is no collusion, and that the petition is not unduly delayed. Before any of that, Section 23(2) obliges the court to make every endeavour to bring about a reconciliation, and Section 23(3) lets it adjourn for up to fifteen days and refer the parties to a person they name. The Family Court's own duty to attempt a settlement, under Section 9 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, sits on top of this. In practice a contested petition at Jodhpur goes to counselling or mediation before issues are framed, and a family should expect that stage rather than resist it.

The six-month cooling-off period, and when it may be waived

The six months in Section 13-B(2) were read as mandatory for many years. In Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, the Supreme Court held the period directory, and set out when a Family Court may dispense with it: where the statutory period of separation under Section 13-B(1), in addition to the six months, is already over before the first motion; where mediation and conciliation have failed; where alimony, custody and property stand settled; and where the waiting period would only prolong the agony. The waiver application is moved a week after the first motion, stating the reasons. Whether to grant it is the Family Court's discretion on the facts of that petition, and a couple should not plan their dates on the assumption that it will be granted. Plan for the six months. Where it is, the second motion is recorded soon after and the decree follows.

Maintenance under Section 144 BNSS, custody, and PWDVA applications alongside the petition

A divorce petition rarely travels alone, and the Family Court at Jodhpur is the forum for most of what travels with it. Inside the petition, Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act provides maintenance pendente lite and the expenses of the proceeding to a spouse who has no independent income sufficient for support, and Section 26 provides for the custody, maintenance and education of minor children, with interim orders available at any stage. Both sections direct that the application be disposed of, as far as possible, within sixty days of service of notice. Outside the petition, a wife, a child or a parent may claim a monthly allowance under Section 144 BNSS, the maintenance provision, which under Section 7(2)(a) of the Family Courts Act is also heard by the Family Court; the same provision is explained in Hindi on गुजारा भत्ता, BNSS धारा 144. Permanent alimony after the decree is a separate order under Section 25. It is not automatic either.

Custody is decided on the welfare of the child, and the Family Court hears it whether it arises inside the divorce petition under Section 26 or as a separate petition under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890; the principles are set out in the note on child custody and guardianship. A woman who has suffered domestic violence may apply to the Magistrate under Section 12 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and Section 27 fixes that Magistrate by where she resides, where the respondent resides, or where the cause of action arose. Section 26 of that Act also allows the protection, residence and monetary reliefs to be sought in a proceeding already before the Family Court, so the two need not run in two buildings. One file, one court. The reliefs themselves are described in the note on applications under the PWDVA, 2005.

Which court at Jodhpur hears the matter, and when it reaches the Rajasthan High Court

Under Section 7(1) of the Family Courts Act, 1984, the Family Court exercises the jurisdiction of the district court for every suit and proceeding between the parties to a marriage for nullity, restitution, judicial separation or dissolution, and for maintenance, guardianship and custody. At Jodhpur that means the Family Court, not the civil judge and not the District Judge. Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act fixes which district: where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides, where the spouses last resided together, or, where the wife is the petitioner, where she resides at presentation. A wife who has returned to her parents at Jodhpur can therefore file here even if the marriage was at Jaipur or Mumbai. Where two petitions are pending in two districts, Section 21-A transfers the later to the court where the earlier was presented; a transfer between States is sought from the Supreme Court under Section 25 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

Section 21-B asks the Family Court to try the petition from day to day and to endeavour to conclude it within six months of service of notice. That is the statute's aspiration; the calendar at Jodhpur is set by the number of matters on the board. The matter reaches the Rajasthan High Court, Principal Seat at Jodhpur, on appeal under Section 19 of the Family Courts Act, both on facts and on law, within thirty days of the judgment or order. Three limits matter. An interlocutory order is not appealable. No appeal lies from a decree passed with the consent of the parties, so a Section 13-B decree is final the day it is passed. There is no second look. And a maintenance order under Section 144 BNSS is not taken in appeal at all; Section 19(4) leaves it to the High Court's revisional power. Section 21-B(3) asks that the appeal be heard within three months of service. The certified copy of the Family Court's judgment has to be on the file before the appeal is presented, and the wait for that copy is routinely the longest part of the thirty days.

Documents a family should gather before the first meeting

  • Proof of the marriage: registration certificate, invitation card, or photographs
  • The date and address where the spouses last lived together
  • Income proof of both sides: salary slips, returns, or bank statements
  • Birth certificates and school records of the children
  • Every notice, complaint, FIR or court order already exchanged
  • A written note of any settlement terms already discussed

The one document that shortens a mutual-consent matter more than any other is the settlement in writing, signed or not. The one that shortens a contested matter is the chronology: a dated list of what happened, prepared before the petition is drafted, because the petition is built on it and the cross-examination will test it. Write it before anything else.

Questions asked most often

How does a mutual-consent divorce under Section 13-B proceed in the Jodhpur Family Court?

Both spouses present one petition stating that they have lived separately for a year or more, cannot live together, and agree to dissolve the marriage. The Family Court at Jodhpur records the first motion. After six months, and within eighteen months of presentation, both appear again for the second motion. If the averments are found true, the decree follows under Section 13-B(2).

When can the six-month cooling-off period be waived?

The Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017) 8 SCC 746 held the six months directory, not mandatory. The Family Court may waive it where the separation already exceeds the statutory period, mediation has failed, alimony, custody and property stand settled, and waiting would only prolong the agony. The application is moved a week after the first motion, with reasons.

Which court in Jodhpur hears a contested divorce petition?

The Family Court at Jodhpur, which under Section 7 of the Family Courts Act, 1984 exercises the jurisdiction of the district court for matrimonial causes. Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act fixes the place: where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent resides, where the parties last lived together, or, for a wife petitioner, where she resides.

Can maintenance or child-custody applications run alongside a divorce petition?

Yes. An application for maintenance pendente lite and litigation expenses under Section 24, and for custody under Section 26, is filed in the divorce petition itself, and each carries a sixty-day disposal direction. A separate claim under Section 144 BNSS also goes to the Family Court. Reliefs under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 may be sought in the same proceeding under Section 26 of that Act.

When does a family matter from Jodhpur go to the Rajasthan High Court?

On appeal under Section 19 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, within thirty days of the judgment or order, to the Principal Seat at Jodhpur. Interlocutory orders are not appealable, and no appeal lies from a decree passed with the consent of the parties. A maintenance order is not appealed; it is examined by the High Court in revision under Section 19(4).

What papers should a family bring to the first meeting at the Ratanada chamber?

Proof of the marriage, such as the registration certificate, the invitation card or photographs; the date and place the spouses last lived together; proof of each side's income; the children's birth certificates; and every notice, FIR, complaint or court order already exchanged. Where a settlement is proposed, a written note of its terms, however rough, saves a hearing.
