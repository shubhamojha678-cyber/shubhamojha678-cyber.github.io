Property Lawyer in Jodhpur: Civil Court or Revenue Court, and the Clocks

A property matter at Jodhpur is settled first by forum and only afterwards by title. Khatedari, mutation and the division of an agricultural holding belong to the revenue courts and climb to the Board of Revenue at Ajmer. Title, possession, injunction and specific performance belong to the civil courts. The Jodhpur Bench comes last, not first.

By Shubham Ojha, BA LL.B., LL.M., ICWA (Foundation) | Advocate, Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur November 2026

Land files reach the chamber at Ratanada in two kinds of folder, and the folder usually tells you more than the grievance does. One carries a jamabandi, a khasra map and a Tehsildar’s order; the other carries a registered sale deed, an agreement to sell and photographs of a boundary wall. The first folder belongs to the revenue courts, the second to the civil courts, and the party holding it has often spent two or three years at the wrong counter before the question was asked. Ask the forum question first. So this page does not argue title. It sets out which door opens, in what order the ladder is climbed, how many days each rung allows, and the point at which a Jodhpur land matter genuinely becomes one for the Rajasthan High Court.

At a glance — which forum takes what
  • Revenue courts — khatedari, mutation, land records, division of an agricultural holding (Section 53), ejectment of a trespasser, declaration of a tenant’s rights (Section 88). Assistant Collector, Sub-Divisional Officer, Tehsildar; then Collector, Revenue Appellate Authority, Board of Revenue at Ajmer.
  • Civil courts — title and declaration, possession, permanent and temporary injunction, specific performance of an agreement to sell, cancellation of a deed, partition of a house or a shop among co-sharers.
  • Rajasthan High Court, Jodhpur Bench — second appeal on a substantial question of law (Section 100 CPC), civil miscellaneous appeal against an injunction order (Order 43 Rule 1(r)), and petitions under Articles 226 and 227 after the revenue ladder has been climbed.

The first question is the forum, not the title

Section 9 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 begins generously: the courts have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature except those of which their cognizance is expressly or impliedly barred. On the property side in Rajasthan the bar is express and it is written twice over. Section 207(1) of the Rajasthan Tenancy Act, 1955 says that all suits and applications of the nature specified in the Third Schedule shall be heard and determined by a revenue court. Section 207(2) then forbids any other court from taking cognizance of such a suit, or of any suit based on a cause of action in respect of which the revenue court could have granted relief.

The sentence that decides the argument is the Explanation to that sub-section: if the cause of action is one in respect of which relief might be granted by the revenue court, it is immaterial that the relief asked for from the civil court is greater than, or additional to, or is not identical with, that which the revenue court could have granted. A khatedari dispute recast as a declaratory suit, with a prayer for possession and damages bolted on, meets that Explanation and stops there. Changing the name of the prayer does not change the door. The bar is not a technicality.

What is less often noticed is that the declaration the party wanted already exists on the revenue side. Section 88 allows a person claiming to be a tenant or a co-tenant to sue for a declaration that he is one, or for a declaration of his share in a joint tenancy; a Khudkasht tenant and a sub-tenant have their own sub-sections. Entry 5 in the Part-Suits table of the Third Schedule places that suit before the Assistant Collector and prescribes no period of limitation for it. The same is true of entry 3, the suit for division of a holding under Section 53. A claim that could not have been filed in the civil court today because it is twenty years old may still be perfectly alive in the revenue court. Age alone kills nothing here.

The revenue ladder, in the order it is actually climbed

Appeals on the pure record side come from the Rajasthan Land Revenue Act, 1956. Section 75(1)(a) takes a first appeal from an original order of a Tehsildar in matters not connected with settlement or land records to the Collector. Section 75(1)(b) takes a first appeal from an original order of an Assistant Collector, a Sub-Divisional Officer or a Collector to the Revenue Appellate Authority. Section 76 then provides the second appeal: from an order passed in appeal by a Collector in matters not connected with settlement or land records, to the Revenue Appellate Authority, and onward to the Board of Revenue, which sits at Ajmer. The Rajasthan Tenancy Act runs its own parallel appeal track for Third Schedule suits, and the two are routinely mixed up in memoranda of appeal; the ladder for those is set out in the Hindi note linked above.

Section 78 is the provision that ends more land matters at Jodhpur than any argument on merits. It fixes three different periods, each counted from the date of the order objected to: thirty days for an appeal to the Collector, the Land Records Officer or the Settlement Officer; sixty days for an appeal to the Revenue Appellate Authority, the Settlement Commissioner or the Director of Land Records; and ninety days for an appeal to the Board. Thirty, sixty, ninety — and the party who assumes that all appeals carry the same period is almost always the one who had thirty. Thirty days is the one most often missed.

Then Section 79, in a single sentence, decides the practical timetable: every petition of appeal must be accompanied by a certified copy of the order objected to, unless its production is dispensed with. The copy comes from the copying branch, not from you. Apply for it on the day the order is pronounced, before the appeal has even been thought through, because Section 78 keeps running while the copy is being prepared. Copy first, appeal afterwards. That single habit saves more revenue appeals here than any drafting skill. Nothing else comes close.

Mutation is a record, not a title

Section 135 of the Land Revenue Act tells the Tehsildar what to do with a reported succession, transfer or other acquisition: make such inquiry as appears necessary and, in undisputed cases, record it in the annual registers. If it is disputed, Section 135(2) requires him to decide it himself where he is competent, and where he is not, to refer the dispute to an officer who is. A party who goes on filing fresh applications before the Tehsildar after the entry has become disputed is waiting at a counter that has already lost the power to decide.

For the smaller problem — a name, a father’s name or a khasra number misprinted in the record — the remedy is not a suit. Section 136 allows the Land Records Officer, at any time, to correct clerical errors and errors which the interested parties admit to have been made in the record of rights or register. Its proviso cuts the other way as well: where an error is noticed by a Revenue Officer during the course of his inspection, nothing may be corrected unless a show-cause notice has been given to the parties. So an admitted error needs an application, and a correction made behind a party’s back is itself a ground of challenge. None of this creates ownership: a mutation is a fiscal entry, and that it confers no title is settled by Balwant Singh v. Daulat Singh (1997). A record is not a title. The mutation procedure itself is set out separately in the note on नामांतरण, अपना खाता और जमाबंदी, and the wider revenue scheme in Rajasthan land revenue law: khasra, khatauni and the revenue courts.

The civil side: injunction, specific performance, possession

On a non-agricultural plot, a house, a shop or a registered transaction, the civil court is the forum and the first relief asked for is almost always a temporary injunction. Order 39 Rule 1 CPC lists the three situations in which it may be granted: property in dispute in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by a party, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decree; a defendant threatening to remove or dispose of his property so as to defraud his creditors; and a defendant threatening to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause him injury in relation to property in dispute in the suit. Rule 2 covers the restraint of a breach of contract or other injury. Rule 2A is the sub-rule that gives an injunction its teeth — on disobedience the court may attach the property of the person in breach and may detain him in civil prison for a term not exceeding three months.

One local point that saves a wasted application. In a suit on the revenue side the temporary injunction does not come from Order 39 at all. Section 212 of the Rajasthan Tenancy Act, 1955 provides for it, on materially narrower grounds — property in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated, or a party threatening to remove or dispose of it in order to defeat the ends of justice — and it also allows the court to appoint a receiver. Section 212(2) then gives the restrained party a route that is almost never used: he may offer cash security in an amount the court determines, and on depositing it the court may withdraw the injunction or the receivership. A party against whom a revenue injunction has been granted and who can find security is not obliged to spend a year appealing against it. Security can buy back possession.

Specific performance of an agreement to sell has changed character, and pleadings drawn on the old understanding still arrive. Section 10 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 as substituted with effect from 1 October 2018 reads that the specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court, subject only to Section 11(2), Section 14 and Section 16. It is no longer a discretionary relief to be prayed for in the alternative to damages. What remains fatal is Section 16(c): specific performance cannot be enforced in favour of a person who fails to prove that he has performed, or has always been ready and willing to perform, the essential terms of the contract on his part. Explanation (i) is worth knowing where money is short — the plaintiff need not actually tender the money to the defendant or deposit it in court unless the court so directs — but Explanation (ii) still requires him to prove readiness and willingness according to the true construction of the contract. Readiness is pleaded and proved, not asserted. The pleading has to say so. The subject is treated at length in the note on specific performance of contracts.

Registration decides what the paper is worth. Section 17(1) of the Registration Act, 1908 makes registration compulsory for instruments of gift of immovable property, for other non-testamentary instruments which purport or operate to create, declare, assign, limit or extinguish any right, title or interest of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards in immovable property, and for leases from year to year or for a term exceeding one year. Section 49 then says that an unregistered document required to be registered shall not affect the immovable property comprised in it, nor be received as evidence of a transaction affecting that property. Parties read that and conclude the paper is dead. The proviso says otherwise: such a document may be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance, or as evidence of a collateral transaction not required to be effected by a registered instrument. What non-registration destroys is the transfer, not the contract — which is exactly why the suit that survives an unregistered agreement to sell is the suit for specific performance and not the suit for declaration of title.

The clocks that lapse quietly

Four periods account for most of the property matters that cannot be filed by the time they are brought in. For specific performance, Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives three years from the date fixed for performance, or, if no such date is fixed, from the date on which the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused. Where the agreement names a date, the clock starts on that date whether or not anyone refused anything, and waiting for a refusal that never comes in writing is the commonest way these suits are lost. For a declaration not otherwise provided for, Article 58 gives three years from when the right to sue first accrues. For possession of immovable property based on title, Article 65 gives twelve years, counted not from the date possession began but from the date on which the defendant’s possession becomes adverse to the plaintiff. And on the revenue side the thirty, sixty and ninety days of Section 78 run from the date of the order, against a party who is usually still waiting for the certified copy.

Against those four, remember the one direction in which Rajasthan law is generous. The Third Schedule prescribes no period of limitation for a suit for division of a holding under Section 53, or for a declaration of a tenant’s rights under Section 88. A joint khata left undivided for thirty or forty years can still be divided today. Section 53(2) recognises only two ways of doing it — by agreement between the co-tenants covering both the division and the distribution of rent over the several portions, or by the decree or order of a competent court in a suit by one or more co-tenants — and Section 53(4) requires every co-tenant and the landholder to be made a party to that suit. Sub-sections (1) and (3) were omitted with effect from 11 November 1992, so a plaint copied from an old precedent is defective on its face. Partition suits here are not lost to limitation. They are lost to a co-sharer who was left out of the array of parties, and to a format nobody checked. The civil-side counterpart, the partition of a house or a shop among co-sharers, is set out in the note on partition suits over family property in Rajasthan.

Where the Jodhpur Bench comes in

Three routes, and none of them is a short cut past the ladder. A second appeal under Section 100 CPC lies to the High Court from every decree passed in appeal by a subordinate court, but only if the High Court is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law. Section 100(3) requires the memorandum of appeal to state that question precisely; Section 100(4) requires the court to formulate it; and under Section 100(5) the appeal is heard on the question so formulated, with the respondent free to argue at the hearing that the case involves no such question. A second appeal drafted as a re-argument of the evidence is answered by the section itself. Formulate the question, or lose it.

A civil miscellaneous appeal is the remedy against an injunction order. Order 43 Rule 1(r) allows an appeal from an order under Rule 1, Rule 2, Rule 2A, Rule 4 or Rule 10 of Order 39, so an order granting or refusing a temporary injunction, or one under Rule 2A, is appealable, and the appeal is presented with its own application for stay. Interim orders on the revenue side are differently placed, and the Hindi page sets out why an appeal does not lie against them.

A petition under Articles 226 and 227 against an order of the Revenue Appellate Authority or the Board of Revenue is heard here, and in practice it survives in three situations: a question of jurisdiction, a breach of natural justice such as a record altered without the show-cause notice that the proviso to Section 136 requires, and an order that no revenue court could have passed at all. Filed in place of the statutory appeal, it is usually returned to the appeal — by which time Section 78 has done its work. The jurisdiction of this Bench extends beyond the district: as our pages on the forums at Jodhpur state it, Barmer, Jaisalmer, Pali, Jalore and Sirohi come here along with Jodhpur, so the revenue orders of the whole Marwar belt are tested in this building. Which of the courts at Jodhpur a matter belongs to in the first place is set out on the chamber page, advocates in Jodhpur and which court hears your matter.

What to bring to a first meeting

  • Jamabandi nakal — a download from Apna Khata is enough
  • Khasra map or trace map, where a boundary is in dispute
  • Certified copy of the order challenged, with its date
  • Registered sale deed, gift deed, will or lease
  • The mutation application and whatever was done on it
  • Names and addresses of all co-khatedars — for Section 53(4)

The one paper without which nothing moves is the certified copy of the order under challenge, because Section 79 requires it to accompany the appeal while Section 78 keeps counting. If it has been applied for and not yet delivered, bring the copy application with its diary number; that is what an application for dispensation is built on. The diary number matters.

Questions asked across the table at Ratanada

The Tehsildar rejected my mutation application. Can I go straight to the High Court at Jodhpur?

No. Section 75(1)(a) of the Rajasthan Land Revenue Act, 1956 sends a first appeal from a Tehsildar’s original order to the Collector, and Section 78(a) allows thirty days from the date of that order. A writ petition filed at this stage is ordinarily sent back to the statutory remedy, and the thirty days are usually gone by the time it comes back.

Can a khatedari dispute be taken to the civil court by calling it a declaratory suit?

No. Section 207(2) of the Rajasthan Tenancy Act, 1955 bars every court other than a revenue court, and its Explanation says it is immaterial that the relief asked from the civil court is greater than, additional to, or not identical with what the revenue court could have granted. The declaration itself exists on the revenue side: Section 88 allows a person claiming to be a tenant or co-tenant to sue for that declaration, or for a declaration of his share in a joint tenancy, and entry 5 of the Part-Suits table in the Third Schedule puts it before the Assistant Collector with no period of limitation.

Our agreement to sell was never registered. Is it worth anything now?

It is not worthless. Section 49 of the Registration Act, 1908 says an unregistered document required to be registered cannot affect the immovable property or be received as evidence of a transaction affecting it, but the proviso then allows it to be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance, or as evidence of a collateral transaction not required to be effected by a registered instrument. What dies with non-registration is the transfer, not the contract.

How long do I have to file a suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell?

Article 54 of the Limitation Act, 1963 gives three years, counted from the date fixed for performance, or, where no such date is fixed, from the date on which the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused. Where the agreement names a date, the clock does not wait for a refusal, and that is the commoner way these suits are lost at Jodhpur.

The civil court refused my application for a temporary injunction. Is that order appealable?

Yes. Order 43 Rule 1(r) CPC allows an appeal from an order under Rule 1, Rule 2, Rule 2A, Rule 4 or Rule 10 of Order 39, so an order granting or refusing a temporary injunction is appealable as a civil miscellaneous appeal, with its own stay application. In a revenue suit the position is different: the injunction there is granted under Section 212 of the Rajasthan Tenancy Act, 1955, not under Order 39.

Jodhpur me zameen ka vakil dhoondh rahe hain — pehle kya saath laana hai?

Jamabandi ki nakal (Apna Khata se download ki hui bhi chalegi), khasra naksha agar seema ka vivad hai, vivadit aadesh ki certified copy with its date, registry ya dan-patra ki copy, aur sabhi co-khatedars ke naam-pate — Section 53(4) ke liye yeh zaroori hai. Jis ek kagaz ke bina kaam rukta hai woh certified copy hai, kyunki Section 79 use appeal ke saath maangta hai.
